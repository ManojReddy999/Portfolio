In production · sole engineer

Growero HRMS

An HR platform for a company with staff spread across regions, many of whom never visit an office. I built and maintain it on my own: about 14,600 lines of TypeScript across 16 application modules, on Next.js 16, React 19, Prisma and PostgreSQL, installable on phones as a progressive web app.

  • Onboarding and directory: employee records with a four-state onboarding workflow, a regional structure and a reporting-manager hierarchy.
  • Geofenced attendance: check-in and check-out coordinates verified against a geofence, plus a location trail per shift, so field visits are auditable rather than self-reported.
  • Leave and expenses: requests with an approval chain; approved expense claims flow into payroll as reimbursements.
  • Documents: payslip PDFs that employees download themselves, and generated offer letters with status tracking.
  • Hand-off: an integration specification (schema, REST endpoints, business logic) so a partner team could rebuild attendance, leave and expenses on their own PHP stack.

The payroll engine

Payroll is the hardest part of an HR system: it is money, it is statutory, and a month that has been paid must never change. The engine:

  • splits CTC into basic, HRA, conveyance, medical, special and vehicle allowances;
  • applies provident fund (fixed or percentage mode), professional tax, ESI, TDS and salary advances;
  • prorates earnings from attendance-derived payable days and loss of pay;
  • moves each payroll run through DRAFT → LOCKED → PAID, so a finalised month cannot drift when someone edits an employee record later.

The commit history reads like a system real people use: serialisation errors that only appear on the hosting platform, dates that shift across the server–browser boundary, time zones, and a missing column that returned errors in production.

FarmInsta Chatbot

Agricultural field officers and their managers ask questions in plain language: a farmer’s visit and product-demo history, which planned visits are still unfinished, how many activities a team completed last month, which farmers need a follow-up after a demonstration. The chatbot answers by querying a read-only copy of the operational database.

  1. Question and context. The question is combined with the last six messages of the conversation, the user’s place in the organisation, and domain-specific SQL instructions.
  2. SQL, then one repair. Gemini writes SQL for the read-only copy. If it fails, the database’s error goes back for one repair attempt.
  3. Answer and follow-ups. Results are formatted into an answer, the supporting SQL is shown, and follow-up questions are suggested. Each turn keeps the question, answer and query, so follow-ups can build on them.

Business definitions in the query layer

Most wrong answers from text-to-SQL come from ambiguity, not syntax. The prompt and its examples pin down distinctions specific to field work:

  • Scheduled vs completed: planned dates describe scheduled visits; submitted status and completion dates describe work done.
  • Farmer vs field officer: a farmer’s history and an officer’s work history use different identities and joins.
  • Current vs historical: team listings filter to active accounts; historical questions keep records from officers who have since left.
  • “My team” vs the whole hierarchy: my team means direct reports; questions about the full hierarchy use recursive SQL.

Built prototype · not deployed

Sales analytics prototype

A separate project from the HRMS: a Python pipeline that loads sales, customer and territory-target spreadsheets into SQLite, with a Streamlit interface for asking questions in plain English. It shows the generated SQL and the result table, so the answer can be checked.

  • 105,359 transaction rows and 14 query indexes across transaction, customer and territory tables.
  • Spreadsheet loading with pandas, including merged headers and reshaped territory targets.
  • April–March fiscal periods, and rules that tell invoices, returns, credit notes and internal transfers apart.

These counts describe the scale of the prototype, not a measured speed-up or adoption. No accuracy or latency evaluation was done.
