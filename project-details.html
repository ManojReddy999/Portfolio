Learning to drive from depth alone

Can a car learn to drive through clutter from a single depth camera, with nothing hand-engineered in between? I built the simulator and the training setup as a graduate researcher in Prof. Nikolay Atanasov’s lab at UC San Diego.

In 2026 I went back to it, audited my own evaluation, rebuilt it, and re-ran the work. This page reports what held up, what didn’t, and the bugs I found in my own simulator.

01

The task

A 1/10-scale MuSHR race car with Ackermann steering starts in a 16 m walled arena scattered with 100–200 obstacles in five shapes. Every 50 ms the policy gets one 64 × 128 depth image from the car’s camera, 79° wide (the image above is a real one), and outputs steering and throttle. The reward is forward speed plus a small bonus for staying alive, and a collision ends the episode.

There is no map and no goal. The policy has to keep moving without crashing, using only what one forward camera shows it. You can drive the same car in the same arena in the lab, with a classical planner standing in for the policy.

02

What I built at UCSD

  • A custom MuJoCo / dm_control environment: the Ackermann car with a simulated RealSense depth camera, randomised obstacle fields, contact-based collision checks, reward and termination.
  • Training with PPO and SAC in Stable-Baselines3, with depth, RGB-D, point-cloud and vehicle-state observations.
  • Three families of feature extractor compared on the same task: a CNN, a point-cloud graph network (k-nearest-neighbour graph with EdgeConv), and a port of LocoTransformer (Yang et al., ICLR 2022) from a quadruped to a car.

03

Why I re-ran it

When I returned to the code in 2026, I audited the evaluation before building on it. It could not support the conclusions I had drawn:

  • The wrong thing was measured. Evaluation never ended an episode on a collision (training did), so “episode length” measured time until the car got stuck, not time until it crashed.
  • A sentinel value was averaged in. Episodes with no collision recorded time-to-first-collision as −1, so the best episodes lowered their own model’s score.
  • One run per model, and a picked checkpoint. The “best” transformer was one checkpoint of one training run; two of its other checkpoints were no better than the graph network.
  • The transformer never attended over time. Stacked frames went in as image channels, so it attended over space only. The comparison never tested temporal attention.

04

How results are judged now

  • Held-out scenes. 100–300 obstacle layouts no policy has trained or been tuned on, identical for every policy so comparisons are paired.
  • Training seeds and intervals. Several training seeds where the question needs them; interquartile means with 95% bootstrap intervals that resample seeds and scenes together (after Agarwal et al., 2021).
  • Rules before results. Each experiment’s pass/fail rules are written down before it runs, including what a single seed cannot decide.
  • Everything written up. Every change and run has a dated write-up and its analysis scripts, kept alongside the code.

05

Results so far

QuestionWhat the evidence says
Does a plain CNN on depth alone beat the earlier models? Yes, on every training seed. About +30 m interquartile mean over the earlier best models, pooled over seeds on 100 fresh scenes, with 2.8–4.2× fewer collisions per metre. That is smaller than the +45 m the first single-seed, 20-scene run suggested.
Does a short stack of recent frames help? Not reliably. Across three seeds the gain was +14.9 m with an interval of −6.8 to +36.9. Where it did help, the policy needed only the newest pair of frames: it was reading motion, not using memory.
Does a reverse gear fix the runs that end stuck? It removes them, for an unexpected reason. Trapped episodes went from 39 of 1,200 to 0 of 900, and stops from 106 to 0. But no policy ever reverses on purpose, and distance didn’t improve. The likely reason, not yet tested: with reverse available, zero throttle stops being a free place to rest.
Why do the policies crash? They don’t brake in time. Replaying all 454 crashes on 100 held-out scenes, braking 0.75 s earlier would have avoided 98–99% of them. Why they don’t learn to brake is open.
Caveat. These were measured on the original simulator, before the fixes below. Comparisons between runs stand; absolute speeds and distances describe the old car.

06

Bugs in my own simulator

  • Every wheel was tilted 26.6°. The wheel collision shapes were written in degrees, but dm_control compiles the model in radians, so 90 meant 90 radians. The car rode on its rim edges, bounced, and was 13% slower. The one-line fix broke steering, because flat tyres scrubbed harder than the steering could turn them, so the real fix rebuilt the wheel contacts.
  • Most of the car couldn’t crash. In the car model I generated, the chassis, camera and LiDAR had collision switched off (the upstream MuSHR model has it on), and the front bumper’s contacts were silently culled because obstacles were moved without updating MuJoCo’s bounding volumes. Only the wheels ever registered a crash, and a car that tipped over could drive on upside down.
  • Brief contacts were missed. Collisions were checked once per 50 ms control step. They now count at every physics substep, and tilting past 70° ends the episode.

All three are fixed, and the corrected car drives identically when upright. Older runs still reproduce exactly on the legacy physics, so earlier results can be re-checked.
